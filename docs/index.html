VerifEye

A browser extension that looks at an image and tells you what its pixels say about where they came from. Hover anything on any page and it reads the physical traces a camera leaves behind — and that a neural decoder does not.

A photograph has a history. Light passed through glass, landed on a sensor behind a colour filter array, was interpolated into full colour, compressed by firmware with a particular quantization table, and probably re-encoded a few times on the way to your screen. Each of those steps leaves a measurable trace.

An image from a diffusion model has none of that history. It was decoded straight to pixels. VerifEye runs eight analyzers looking for the traces, weighs what it finds, and reports a verdict along with how much it actually knows.

Everything runs locally. In the default mode there are no network calls, no uploads and no API key. The engine has no runtime dependencies.

Read the probabilities as a ranking, not as likelihoods. The fusion weights ship hand-set rather than fitted to labelled data, so a 72% is more suspicious than a 44% — but it is not “72% likely to be AI”. Calibration explains how to fix that, and why it has not been done for you.

Installation

Works in Chrome, Brave, Edge and Arc. There is no store listing yet, so load it unpacked.

$ git clone https://github.com/zihaaaad/VerifEye.git
$ cd VerifEye
  1. Open chrome://extensions.
  2. Turn on Developer mode — the toggle sits in the upper right.
  3. Click Load unpacked and choose the folder you just cloned.
  4. Click the toolbar icon to switch detection on, then hover any image.

Hovering gives you a fast read. Press V while the tooltip is showing to run a deep scan: it works at higher resolution, performs a real recompression round trip for error level analysis, and draws the measured frequency spectrum into the tooltip.

    The signals

    Eight analyzers, each reading something different. Seven contribute to the verdict. One is measured, displayed, and deliberately ignored — explained below.

    SignalWhat it readsWeight
    metadata PNG tEXt/iTXt chunks, JPEG APP segments, XMP, EXIF and C2PA manifests. Finds Automatic1111 parameters, ComfyUI graphs, Midjourney flags, and camera bodies. Decisive
    fft Fourier transform over Hann-windowed luminance tiles, hunting the periodic harmonics that transposed convolutions leave behind, plus the radial power-law slope against the natural falloff. 1.00
    cfa Bayer demosaicing traces. See below. 1.10
    noise Laplacian high-pass residual, kurtosis measured against the Gaussian 3.0, and patch-wise detection of the oversmoothing diffusion models produce on skin and sky. 0.90
    ela Re-encodes at a known quality and differences the result. Regions with a different compression history — a splice, an inpainted patch — surface as error-level outliers. 0.60
    color Inter-channel correlation, saturation entropy, and the radial chromatic fringing that physical glass produces at peripheral high-contrast edges. 0.50
    jpegQuant Parses JPEG quantization tables and matches them against the IJG reference at every quality setting. Cameras and Adobe ship custom tables; Pillow and libjpeg ship the standard one, scaled. 0.40
    benford Generalized Benford divergence over block-DCT coefficients. Does not vote

    Why the demosaicing signal only argues one way

    A single-sensor camera does not measure colour at every pixel. It measures one channel per photosite through a colour filter array and reconstructs the other two by interpolation. In the common Bayer arrangement, green is sampled on one diagonal parity of the 2×2 cell and interpolated on the other.

    Interpolated pixels are, by construction, near-exact averages of their neighbours. So a bilinear prediction residual nearly vanishes on half the image and stays large on the other half. That asymmetry is hard evidence of an optical sensor. A neural decoder emits all three channels at every pixel and leaves both parities identical.

    The absence of a lattice proves nothing. Any resize, any crop to an odd offset, any re-encode destroys it too — which describes most images on the web. So this signal is permitted to argue that something is a photograph, and structurally forbidden from arguing the reverse. The fusion layer clamps it, and a test asserts the clamp holds.

    Why one signal is measured but ignored

    Leading digits of block-DCT coefficients in natural photographs follow a generalized Benford distribution closely. It is a well-documented result and an appealing signal to add. It is implemented here, and it does not vote.

    Tested against 1/f fractional-Brownian references — the closest available stand-in for natural-image statistics without a labelled corpus — its divergence tracked grain amplitude and JPEG quality far more strongly than it tracked whether content was synthetic. The ordering even inverted between quality settings: at quality 85, an oversmoothed surface fit the law better than a natural one.

    An uncalibrated statistic that behaves like that would add noise to the verdict while looking authoritative. It stays visible, labelled as diagnostic. If a calibration run ever shows real separation, it can be given a weight.

    How signals combine

    Signals are combined in log-odds rather than averaged:

    logit(P) = prior + Σ weight_i × evidence_i      evidence_i ∈ [−1, +1]
P        = sigmoid(logit)

    Averaging percentages is the wrong operation for combining evidence. Two independent signals each reporting 70% ought to move a verdict further than either one alone, but their mean is still 70. Accumulating log-odds moves it: in practice one signal at 70 yields 60%, and two yield 68%.

    It also lets a one-sided signal contribute in only the direction it is entitled to, and lets a signal that could not be evaluated drop out entirely instead of being folded in at a neutral value, which would quietly dilute everything else toward 50%.

    Reading the output

    Every verdict carries three numbers beyond the headline percentage.

    Band
    A plausible range. It widens when signals disagree with each other, and when evidence could not be gathered at all. A bare percentage hides both conditions; this is the honest half of the verdict.
    Agreement
    How much the contributing signals concur. Low agreement with a high score means one analyzer is shouting while the others shrug — worth reading the breakdown before believing it.
    Coverage
    How much of the total available evidence weight was actually usable on this image. A heavily recompressed image loses its lattice and its original quantization table, so coverage drops. That is the tool telling you it has less to go on than usual.

    The contribution breakdown shows which signals moved the verdict and by how much, in log-odds, signed toward whichever conclusion they supported. It is there so you can disagree with a verdict on specific grounds rather than in general.

    Resolution

    Some evidence only survives at native resolution. Rescaling resamples every pixel, which erases the demosaicing lattice and shifts the JPEG block grid off its original phase. An analyzer reading those from a scaled buffer measures the resize, not the image.

    So every image is decoded into two views: a bounded working buffer for the analyzers that tolerate scaling, and an 8-pixel-aligned centre crop at true resolution for the ones that do not. Holding the crop to multiples of 8 preserves the original block boundaries.

    Signals that depend on the lattice report themselves as uninformative when that crop is unavailable, rather than returning a reading they cannot support.

    Calibration

    The shipped weights are hand-set priors. They reflect a judgement about how much each signal is worth; they are not coefficients fitted to labelled data. Every verdict reports calibrated: false and the interface says so.

    To replace them with something fitted:

    # 1. Collect signal vectors from labelled images, in the browser
$ npx serve .
  open bench/calibrate.html, drop in real photos and known-AI
  images, then export corpus.json

# 2. Fit logistic weights and report held-out AUC
$ npm run calibrate -- bench/corpus.json
$ npm run sync

    The collector stores only numeric signal scores — no pixels, no filenames — so a corpus file can be shared or inspected without redistributing anyone’s images.

    The fitter refuses to write weights that fail to beat chance on a held-out split. A fit that only works on its own training data would flip the calibrated flag to true and invite everything downstream to trust a number that had not earned it. Weights are also specific to the corpus they came from: one drawn from a single generator or a single camera will not transfer.

    Engine modes

    ModeBehaviour
    local default Entirely offline. No network calls of any kind.
    hybrid Local signals run first. Gemini is consulted only when provenance has not already settled the question, and the two are blended 40/60.
    cloud Gemini multimodal evaluation, with the local signal readings still reported alongside it.

    Hybrid and cloud need a Gemini API key, set in the options page and stored in extension storage. Local mode transmits nothing.

    Performance

    Measured rather than asserted. Run npm run bench; results are written to bench/RESULTS.md.

    Input sizeFull ensemble, median
    256 × 256~7 ms
    384 × 384 hover default~9 ms
    768 × 768 deep scan~21 ms

    This is engine time only — decoded pixels in, verdict out. It excludes network fetch, image decode and browser scheduling, which usually dominate what you actually experience on hover. Treat it as a floor, not an end-to-end promise. It says nothing at all about accuracy; that needs the calibration corpus above.

    Development

    $ npm test        # 49 assertions, plus the docs-mirror drift check
$ npm run bench   # latency measurements
$ npm run sync    # mirror forensics/ into docs/forensics/

    GitHub Pages serves this site from docs/, and a page there cannot import from a parent directory, so the engine genuinely exists twice on disk. tools/sync-forensics.mjs is the only writer of that mirror, and npm test fails if it has drifted — otherwise a stale mirror would ship a demo that behaves differently from the extension. 

    forensics/          the engine — pure functions, no DOM, no dependencies
  ensemble.js         orchestration and the provenance short-circuit
  fusion.js           log-odds combination
  calibration.js      fitted weights, or an honest "not calibrated"
  decode.js           working buffer + native-resolution crop

content.js          hover detection and the tooltip
docs/               this site, plus the studio's Web Worker
bench/              benchmark, corpus collector, calibration fitter

    Limitations

    Stated plainly, because a forensics tool that oversells itself is worse than no tool at all.

    • Not calibrated out of the box. Probabilities rank images; they are not likelihoods.
    • Recompression destroys evidence. An image that has been through a social platform has usually lost its demosaicing lattice and its original quantization table. Expect the band to widen and coverage to fall.
    • Screenshots of real photographs read as synthetic to several signals, because they genuinely are re-rendered pixels.
    • Nothing here is adversarially robust. Anything that knows about these measurements can defeat them: add plausible grain, re-encode with a camera quantization table, resample to kill the lattice.
    • A “Likely AI” verdict is evidence, not proof. Use it to decide what deserves a closer look. Never use it as the sole basis for a claim about a person.
